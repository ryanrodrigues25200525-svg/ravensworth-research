## Executive summary

Micron is the market's highest-conviction AI-memory name, and the framing that made it
cheap for a decade — a cyclical, low-multiple commodity producer that lost money in
fiscal 2023, whose ASPs swing 40–50% a year, and that trades on price-to-book because
"memory is a widget" — is now half wrong. Something genuinely structural has changed:
roughly 20% of Micron's DRAM volume and 30% of its NAND volume is locked into 16
multi-year Strategic Customer Agreements worth close to $100bn in remaining performance
obligations, priced at floors management says exceed prior-cycle *peak* margins, with
roughly $18bn in customer cash deposits already posted. And HBM's worsening wafer
"trade ratio" — estimated to rise from ~18% of DRAM wafer input (end-2025) to ~30%
(2027) — means every unit of HBM capacity physically removes non-HBM DRAM supply, a
constraint that didn't exist in 2018 or 2022. That is a real, evidenced reason DRAM and
NAND ASPs are up 130–140% this fiscal year on barely any volume growth.

But the stock has already re-rated for it — hard. Micron trades around $1,012, roughly
ten times its January-2024 level, with a trailing P/E of 22.9x and a price-to-book of
11.3x that has no precedent at a memory-cycle peak (historically 2–5x). The much-quoted
6.5x forward P/E is a peak-earnings artifact, not a valuation. Benchmarked correctly —
against SK Hynix, the closest structural comp, on EV/EBITDA rather than against
AI-infrastructure names like Broadcom — Micron trades at more than twice SK Hynix's
multiple despite near-identical EBITDA margins.

**Our call is one specific, falsifiable disagreement with the Street.** The Street is
overwhelmingly Buy (43 analysts, median target $1,550) and is implicitly underwriting a
re-rating toward AI-infrastructure multiples (Broadcom at 45.5x). We anchor the exit
multiple to SK Hynix's 7.7x. Probability-weighted (25% bear / 45% base / 30% bull), that
disagreement is worth: **Hold, target $940 (~7% below spot).**

## Investment thesis

**The bull case — the structural claims:**

1. **Contracts de-risk the downside.** 20% of DRAM and 30% of NAND volume sits in 16
   Strategic Customer Agreements running through calendar 2030, take-or-pay, worth close
   to $100bn in remaining performance obligations, priced at floors above prior-cycle
   *peak* margins. That is a revenue floor that does not move with spot pricing — the
   closest thing memory has ever had to contracted backlog.
2. **HBM's wafer trade ratio is a physical supply cut, not a management promise.** As
   the top three push toward an estimated 30% of total DRAM wafer input into HBM by 2027,
   non-HBM DRAM supply is mechanically capped even if everyone wanted to overbuild. This
   is a constraint that did not exist in 2018 or 2022 and it is visible in the pricing: DRAM
   ASPs up "low 60s%" sequentially on bit shipments up only low-single-digits.
3. **Pricing this cycle is scarcity-driven, not unit-driven.** DRAM prices were up
   roughly 30% quarter-on-quarter industry-wide in Q2 2026; Micron reported DRAM ASPs up
   "low 60s percent" sequentially, NAND ASPs up "mid-80s," on low-to-mid-single-digit bit
   growth. The cleanest evidence that the cycle is being priced by availability, not by
   units moving.
4. **The margin proof is on the record.** Gross margin went 35.3% → 84.6% in seven
   quarters through fiscal Q3 2026, with operating margins (80.4% GAAP) now 10–15 points
   above NVIDIA (65.6%) and TSMC (60.3%). Micron disclosed its first hard HBM number this
   quarter — over $1bn of HBM4 revenue — with the 12-high HBM4 ramp tracking roughly twice
   the pace HBM3E managed a year earlier.

**The bear case — the cyclical claims:**

5. **The multiple already knows.** The stock is roughly ten times its January-2024 level
   at 11.3x book — a valuation with no precedent at a memory-cycle peak — and trades at
   more than twice SK Hynix's EV/EBITDA despite near-identical EBITDA margins. Paying for
   the structural thesis is one thing; paying 11x book for a company that has round-tripped
   earnings before is another.
6. **Micron's own history argues for humility.** The stock fell 56% in seven months after
   its 2018 peak and 51% in eight months after its 2022 peak — both times on a supply
   response nobody had underwritten. Peak frameworks have failed here twice in a decade.
7. **The precondition for the next glut is forming.** All three producers are raising
   record capex into record margins simultaneously — SK Hynix's $720bn multi-year buildout,
   Samsung pushing toward full utilization plus new Pyeongtaek lines, Micron's own step-up
   from ~$27bn (FY2026) to "above the mid-$40s billion range" (FY2027). The bull answer is
   the contract structure; it has never actually been tested, because this cycle hasn't
   turned yet.

**The falsifiable question.** The bull case rests entirely on the SCA/long-term-agreement
structure holding through a full down-cycle — something that has never happened, because
this cycle is still running. The gap between our $940 and the Street's $1,550 is exactly
that judgment call: contract floors plus trade-ratio constraints versus a history of
multi-year memory gluts and a stock that already re-rated. If the broader re-rating toward
AI-infrastructure multiples holds, the Street is right and our bull case ($1,425) is the
relevant floor, not the ceiling.

## Sector analysis — DRAM, NAND, and HBM

DRAM is produced by three companies controlling over 90% of the market: Samsung
(~38.5% share), SK Hynix (~28.8%), and Micron (~22.4%) — TrendForce data, Q1 2026.
HBM is made by exactly the same three, and none of them discloses HBM revenue as
a reported line, so every HBM "market share" number in circulation, including the
ones below, is a third-party bit-shipment estimate. Counterpoint Research put
Q1 2026 HBM revenue share at SK Hynix 58%, Samsung 21%, Micron 21%; a more recent
UBS 2027 bit-share forecast shows the gap narrowing sharply — Samsung ~41%,
SK Hynix ~39% — as Samsung's faster HBM4 certification bites into SK Hynix's lead.
Micron is the clear third player in the highest-margin tier of its own core
business, trying to close a gap against two much larger competitors during the
exact product transition (HBM4) that decides who wins the next three years.

**What's different this cycle.** In 2018 and 2022, memory makers built ahead of
demand on spot-price extrapolation; when hyperscaler orders normalized, ASPs fell
40–50% and pushed SK Hynix to a negative net margin in 2023. This time: Micron has
16 Strategic Customer Agreements running through calendar 2030, take-or-pay, with
roughly $18bn in customer cash deposits already posted; SK Hynix has finalized
around ten long-term agreements; Samsung is targeting 60–70% of capacity under
long-term contract. And HBM's trade ratio is a physical constraint, not a
management promise — the top three's HBM wafer input as a share of total DRAM
wafer input is estimated to rise from ~18% (end-2025) to ~30% (2027), mechanically
capping non-HBM supply growth even if everyone wanted to overbuild.

**What hasn't changed.** All three producers are now spending record capex into
record margins at the same time — SK Hynix's $720bn multi-year buildout, Samsung
pushing toward full utilization plus new Pyeongtaek lines, Micron's own capex
step-up from ~$27bn (FY2026) to "above the mid-$40s billion range" (FY2027). That
is, by the sector's own history, the precise setup that preceded both the 2019 and
2023 gluts. The bull case rests entirely on the SCA/long-term-agreement structure
holding through a full down-cycle — something that has never actually been tested,
because this cycle hasn't turned yet.

**Margins, for context.** Operating margins in the most recent reported quarter:
Micron 80.4% (GAAP), SK Hynix 76.3%, Samsung Device Solutions 70.0% — all
10–15 points above NVIDIA's 65.6% and TSMC's 60.3% in the same period. DRAM prices
were up roughly 30% quarter-on-quarter industry-wide in Q2 2026; Micron itself
reported DRAM ASPs up in the "low 60s percent" range sequentially on bit shipments
up only low-single-digits — the cleanest evidence that this cycle is priced by
scarcity, not by units moving.

**HBM roadmap vs. SK Hynix and Samsung.** Micron's HBM4 (36GB, 12-high) is built

validation and is guiding to more than 60% of its own HBM mix from HBM4 in the
second half of 2026. All three report capacity effectively sold out through
calendar 2027.

## Model

Nine months of fiscal 2026 revenue (through Q3) totaled $78,959m; the Q4 guide of
$50.0bn ± $1.0bn puts full-year FY2026 revenue at roughly $129–131bn, about 3.5x
FY2025. We build the model on price × contracted volume rather than a unit-CAGR,
because management has said growth is almost entirely ASP-driven this year and
because the SCA structure (20% of DRAM / 30% of NAND volume at floor prices
through FY2030) provides a revenue floor that doesn't move with spot pricing.
Capex intensity actually *fell* through FY2026 as revenue outgrew spending —
39.5% of sales in Q1, 26.8% in Q2, 18.9% in Q3 — before the FY2027 step-up to the
mid-$40s billions, which at a plausible $170–200bn FY2027 revenue base would still
sit in the low-to-mid 20s percent of sales. Trailing-twelve-month EBITDA, built
bottom-up from XBRL operating income plus D&A, is $68.3bn against an enterprise
value of $1.12tn — 16.4x TTM EV/EBITDA.

## Valuation

We use EV/NTM EBITDA as the primary lens, not P/E — a company whose earnings
round-tripped from a $5.8bn annual net loss in fiscal 2023 to a peak-cycle run
rate three years later should not be valued on a multiple of that peak number.

## Risks — ranked

1. **Memory price cyclicality.** Micron's own 10-Q discloses that DRAM ASPs have
   swung from +40% to −48% annually over the past five years, and NAND from +30%
   to −52%. This year's move — +140% DRAM, +130% NAND — is the largest in that
   disclosed range in either direction, meaning a comparably violent reversal has
   precedent every cycle. All three producers are raising capex into record
   margins simultaneously right now, the same setup that preceded the 2019 and
   2023 gluts. The SCA structure is the bull's answer, but it has not been tested
   through an actual down-cycle. **Tell:** DRAM channel inventory data, any SCA
   renegotiation headlines, and whether ASP growth decelerates faster than the
   already-moderating Q4 guide implies.
2. **HBM share loss to SK Hynix and Samsung.** Micron holds an estimated 21–22%
   of HBM revenue against SK Hynix's 58%, and UBS's 2027 forecast has Samsung
   (41%) and SK Hynix (39%) both ahead of Micron in the next-gen product — Samsung
   because it certified HBM4 first. **Tell:** NVIDIA's HBM4 supplier qualification
   announcements and each company's disclosed HBM mix; Micron's own stated target
   is HBM share matching its ~22% DRAM share — any print below that is negative.
3. **Hyperscaler capex digestion.** Data-center revenue is now Micron's largest
   and fastest-growing exposure, effectively a derivative of four or five
   hyperscalers' capex plans. SCA floor pricing protects margin on contracted
   volume, not incremental demand above it. **Tell:** hyperscaler capex language
   shifting from "accelerating" to "normalizing," or GPU order push-outs, which
   would hit HBM orders first since HBM ships bundled with GPU modules.
4. **China exposure.** Micron's 10-Q names trade restrictions and tariffs as a
   risk factor; management explicitly excluded geopolitical impacts from its Q4
   guidance, meaning the guide has a known blind spot here. China's CXMT is
   scaling domestic DRAM/NAND with state backing and isn't export-control
   constrained the way logic fabs are. **Tell:** new export-control rules naming
   HBM or advanced packaging equipment, or CXMT capacity disclosures.
5. **Capex execution risk.** FY2027 capex more than doubles to fund Idaho, New
   York, and Singapore capacity that only contributes meaningfully from mid-2027
   onward — a multi-year window where capital is committed well ahead of
